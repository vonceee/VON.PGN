The Opera Game

Morphy vs. Duke of Brunswick & Count Isouard – Paris, 1858

Paris, 1858. Paul Morphy is at the opera, seated in a private box, playing chess without looking away from the stage for long. His opponents, the Duke of Brunswick and Count Isouard, are consulting together on the other side of the board. Neither the setting nor the opposition matters much in hindsight — what survives is seventeen moves that read like a short lesson in why development wins games.

Grabbing the center

The game opens plainly: 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 Bg4?!. Black's pin on the knight looks natural, but it costs a tempo Morphy is happy to exploit.

4. dxe5 strikes the center pawn, and after Bxf3 White recaptures with 5. Qxf3, arriving on f3 with the bishop pair, an open center, and a lead in development that never really goes away.

Development over material

dxe5 6. Bc4 Nf6 7. Qb3 — Morphy keeps bringing pieces out while his opponents shuffle their queen. By move nine both sides look reasonable, but only one side is actually finishing development.

The sacrifice & finish

7... Qe7 8. Nc3 c6 9. Bg5 b5 10. Nxb5! cxb5 11. Bxb5+ Nbd7 12. O-O-O Rd8 13. Rxd7! Rxd7 14. Rd1 Qe6 15. Bxd7+ Nxd7 16. Qb8+! Nxb8 17. Rd8# — A brilliant queen sacrifice finishes the game in mate with only rook and bishop remaining active.
